\section{Logistic regression meta-classifier}

Now that we have computed the decision functions for several kernels, we can combine their results. Rather than discarding the magnitude of $f(\textbf x)$ with a binary threshold, we use the continuous values directly.

\subsection{10-dimensional representation}

For each antibody chain $\textbf x$, we build a 10-dimensional vector using the output of 10 distinct SVM kernel tests:
$$
\textbf v(\textbf x) = \bigl(f_1(\textbf x),\, f_2(\textbf x),\, \dots,\, f_{10}(\textbf x)\bigr) \in \mathbb R^{10},
$$
where the components correspond to:
\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $f_1$: Cauchy
    \item $f_2$: Gaussian (RBF)
    \item $f_3$: Linear
    \item $f_4$: Sigmoid
    \item $f_5$: Tanimoto
    \item $f_6$: Piecewise ($C=0.80$, $W=0.20$)
    \item $f_7$: Piecewise ($C=0.80$, $W=0.08$)
    \item $f_8$: Piecewise ($C=0.82$, $W=0.08$)
    \item $f_9$: Piecewise ($C=0.84$, $W=0.08$)
    \item $f_{10}$: Piecewise ($C=0.78$, $W=0.08$)
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}

Before fitting our model, we standardize each feature using the mean $\mu_k$ and standard deviation $\sigma_k$ measured on the validation dataset:
$$
\hat f_k(\textbf x) = \frac{f_k(\textbf x) - \mu_k}{\sigma_k}.
$$

\subsection{Model and training}

We define the linear combination:
$$
z(\textbf x) = b + \sum_{k=1}^{10} w_k\, \hat f_k(\textbf x),
$$
where $w_k$ is the weight of the $k$-th kernel and $b$ is the bias. The probability of being human is obtained through the logistic function:
$$
P(\text{Human} \mid \textbf x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z(\textbf x)}}.
$$
If $P(\text{Human} \mid \textbf x) \ge 0.5$, we classify the chain as human ($+1$), and as murine ($-1$) otherwise.

We train the model on the validation dataset (667 samples) by minimizing the L2-regularized binary cross-entropy with gradient descent:
$$
\mathcal L(\textbf w, b) = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \Bigl[y_i \log \hat y_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat y_i)\Bigr] + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{10} w_k^2,
$$
where $y_i \in \{0,1\}$, $\hat y_i = P(\text{Human} \mid \textbf x_i)$, $\lambda = 0.01$, and learning rate $\eta = 0.5$ over 2000 iterations.

\subsection{Permutation importance}

Because several of the evaluated kernels have strong mutual correlations, the regression weights get distributed among them and do not necessarily show how much each kernel contributes on its own. 

To measure the independent importance of each feature, we compute the permutation importance on the test dataset: for each kernel, we randomly permute its column across all 668 test sequences 500 times, breaking its association with the true tag, and measure the resulting decrease in accuracy and F1 scores.

\subsection{Comparison with decision tree (CART)}

As an alternative approach, we also tested a decision tree (CART algorithm) trained on 10-dimensional vectors. A comparison of the test dataset metrics shows:

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
    \begin{tabular}{lcccc} \cline{2-5}
        Model & Accuracy & F1 Human & F1 Murine & $\mathcal Q$ \\ \hline \hline
        CART (depth 5) & $0.9656$ & $0.9714$ & $0.9568$ & $0.9131$ \\ \hline
        Logistic regression & $0.9865$ & $0.9890$ & $0.9827$ & $0.9850$ \\ \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Test dataset results comparing the decision tree and logistic regression models.}
    \label{tab:logreg_comparison}
\end{table}

Incorporating the fine-tuned $W=0.08$ piecewise kernels ($C \in \{0.78, 0.80, 0.82, 0.84\}$) alongside the standard global kernels boosts test accuracy to $98.65\%$ and $\text{F1}_\text{H}$ to $0.9890$. Because the decision function outputs are continuous and provide complementary estimates of humanness, a regularized linear combination achieves a reliable separation without the overfitting associated with orthogonal tree partitions.
