\section{Support Vector Machine}

We intend to back up the humanness of the chain outputted by the simulated annealing algorithm with this binary-output method.

The main point of this method is to transform our data space into some other one. In such a way that we can separate the data points with a simple plane, a linear classifier.

We will represent each point with a $n$-dimensional vector, where $n$ is the number of data points. The function that gives us those coordinates is called the kernel.

\subsection{The kernel}

Our kernel, $\operatorname k:\mathcal C\times\mathcal C\to \mathbb R$,  will be the Gaussian radial basis function: $$\operatorname k(\textbf x_i, \textbf x_j)=\exp \left( 
    -\gamma \left\| \textbf x_i -\textbf x_j \right\|^2 
\right),\ \gamma>0,$$ where $\{\textbf x_i\}$ are the data points.

After applying the kernel we have a matrix $M\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$, the Gram matrix, with the coordinates of our data points in the new space. That will be: $[M]_{i,j}=\operatorname k(\textbf{x}_i,\textbf{x}_j)$.

We also have abailable some other alternative kernel functions:

\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sigmoid function:
    \begin{equation*}
        \operatorname k(\textbf{x} _i,\textbf{x} _j)=\tanh \Big[ \gamma \big( 
            \left\langle \textbf{x} _i| \textbf{x} _j \right\rangle
            +r 
         \big) 
         \Big]
    \end{equation*}
    
    \item Polynomial inhomogenuous function: \begin{equation*}
        \operatorname k(\textbf{x} _i,\textbf{x} _j)=\big( 
            \left\langle \textbf{x} _i| \textbf{x} _j \right\rangle
            +r 
         \big) ^d
    \end{equation*}
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
Being $d>0$ and $r$ real constants.

The Gram matrix and the kernel are the tools we need to craft the decision function.

\subsection{Decision function}

Using the lagrange multipliers method for minimization\futnotetext\footnotetext{See apendices for full explaination.}, we get the decision function, $f:\mathcal C\to\mathbb R$:
$$
f(\textbf x) = b + \sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i s_i \operatorname k(\textbf x_i,\textbf x),
$$
where $\lambda_i$ are the lagrange multipliers obtained through said method, $s_i$ is the \textit{tag} of the $i$-th data point (may be $+1$, when human, or $-1$, when murine) and $b$ is the bias, obtained as follows:
$$
b=s_p-\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i s_i \operatorname k(\textbf x_i, \textbf x_p),
$$
where the $p$-th data point is a support one, that is, the closest to the plane (or any of them) and such that $\lambda_p>0$.

Counting human as positive, this gets us to a F1\futnotetext\footnotetext{$\text{F1}=\frac{2\cdot \text{TP} }{2\cdot \text{TP}+\text{FN}+\text{FP}   }$} of $\sim 0.9753$\futnotetext\footnotetext{Precisely: $\text{F1}_{\text{H}}=0.975259949802797$.}. However, those tests were taken with a completely heuristic upper limit, we will call $\lambda_\text{t} $, for the $\lambda$ vector. Since we cannot determine annalytically $\lambda_\text{t} $, we must adjust it as a hyperparameter with a validation data set.

\subsubsection{Validation}
We have merged all data sets and split them into \texttt{learn}, \texttt{validation}, and \texttt{test}, with $70\%$, $15\%$, and $15\%$ of the chains respectively.

Through this method we get an optimal value for $\lambda_\text{t}=10.0$, having tried with powers of $10$ from $0.01$ to $1000$. Using that hyperparameter, we get the following confusion matrix
\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
    \begin{tabular}{ccc} \cline{2-3}
        & Human & Murine \\ \hline
    Tagged as human    & 404 & 4 \\ \hline
    Tagged as murine    & 6 & 254 \\ \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

And F1 values and accuracy:
\begin{equation*}
    \text{F1}_\text{H} = 0.9878\futnotemath\footnotetext{Precisely: $\text{F1}_\text{H} = 0.987775061124694$.}  \qquad
    \text{F1}_\text{M} = 0.9788\futnotemath\footnotetext{Precisely: $\text{F1}_\text{M} =0.978805394990366$.}  \qquad
    \text{Acc} = 0.9836\futnotemath\footnotetext{Precisely: $\text{Acc}=0.98355754857997 $} 
\end{equation*}
