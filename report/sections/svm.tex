\section{Support Vector Machine}

We intend to back up the humanness of the chain outputted by the simulated annealing algorithm with this binary-output method.

The main point of an SVM (abbreviation of Support Vector Machine) is to transform our data space into some other one in such a way that we can separate the data points with a simple hyperplane, a linear classifier.

We will represent each point with a $n$-dimensional vector, where $n$ is the number of data points. The function that gives us those coordinates is called the kernel.

\subsection{The kernel}

Our kernel, $\operatorname k:\mathcal C\times\mathcal C\to \mathbb R$,  will be the Gaussian radial basis function: $$\operatorname k(\textbf x_i, \textbf x_j)=\exp \left( 
    -\gamma \left\| \textbf x_i -\textbf x_j \right\|^2 
\right),\ \gamma>0,$$ where $\{\textbf x_i\}$ are the data points.

After applying the kernel we have a matrix $M\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$, the Gram matrix, with the coordinates of our data points in the new space. That will be: $[M]_{i,j}=\operatorname k(\textbf{x}_i,\textbf{x}_j)$.

We also have abailable some other alternative kernel functions:\futnotetext\footnotetext{These constants ($298$ and $596=2\cdot 298$) that are scattered around the functions seem to be arbitrary, but the written functions are already optimized for our use case: they are normalized and centered so that every output is positive and does not surpass 1.}

\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sigmoid function:
    \begin{equation*}
        \operatorname k(\textbf{x} _i,\textbf{x} _j)=\tanh \frac{
            \left\langle \textbf{x} _i| \textbf{x} _j \right\rangle
        }{
            298
        }
    \end{equation*}

    \item Tanimoto function:
    \begin{equation*}
        \operatorname k(\textbf{x} _i,\textbf{x} _j)=\frac{
            \left\langle \textbf{x} _i| \textbf{x} _j \right\rangle
        }{596 - \left\langle \textbf{x} _i| \textbf{x} _j \right\rangle}
    \end{equation*}
    
    \item Linear function: \begin{equation*}
        \operatorname k(\textbf{x} _i,\textbf{x} _j)=
        \frac{\left\langle \textbf{x} _i| \textbf{x} _j \right\rangle}{298}
    \end{equation*}

    \item Cauchy function:
    \begin{equation*}
        \operatorname k(\textbf{x} _i,\textbf{x} _j)=\frac{
            298
        }{298 + \left\| \textbf{x}_i-\textbf{x}_j   \right\|^2 }
    \end{equation*}
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}



The Gram matrix and the kernel are the tools we need in order to craft the decision function.

\subsection{Decision function}

Using the lagrange multipliers method for minimization\futnotetext\footnotetext{See apendices for full explaination.}, we get the decision function, $f:\mathcal C\to\mathbb R$:
$$
f(\textbf x) = b + \sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i s_i \operatorname k(\textbf x_i,\textbf x),
$$
where $\lambda_i$ are the lagrange multipliers obtained through said method, $s_i$ is the \textit{tag} of the $i$-th data point (may be $+1$, when human, or $-1$, when murine) and $b$ is the bias, obtained as follows:
$$
b=s_p-\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i s_i \operatorname k(\textbf x_i, \textbf x_p),
$$
where the $p$-th data point is a support one, that is, the closest to the plane (or any of them) and such that $\lambda_p>0$.

We can infere how to evaluate a mixture of the five tests using how they are correlated to eachother. If there is a high correlation index between two tests (like the Gaussian and Cauchy kernels), then we know they are similar and it will not make a difference to ignore one of the two. However, if there is a low correlation index, most likely, we will see that the datapoints one of the tests get wrong the other one will get right and vice-versa.
